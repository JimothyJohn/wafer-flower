Why T3 is the gate
Gravity shear on the bond is 0.13 kPa against a ~500 kPa capability — a ~4000× margin. The load case that matters is the 10–30 °C swing driving 110 µm of differential CTE slip at the land's far corner, ~47× the gravity stress and rising linearly with land size. A compliant bond absorbs it as ~10 % shear strain; a rigid bond puts it into the silicon. T3 with real thermal cycles is the only test that proves your specific adhesive on your specific print surface. Full-surface coverage is actively harmful: area was never the limit, and longer bonds see more end slip. The full analysis is OP 015 on the engineering page.
OP 020

Bond — bench, one station at a time

  1. IPA-wipe land and wafer contact zone; let flash.
  2. Tape path: two 20–25 mm strips arced on the land, ≥5 mm inside its boundary, as close to the land centroid as the geometry allows (OP 015 §4: stress scales with distance from centroid, not with area). Roller-press. Liners off only with wafer in hand. Liquid path (SMP): serpentine bead, 5 mm inside the land edge; Ø4 × #bond printed nubs (3×) set the bondline.
  3. Rest wafer edge on the jig's downhill fence first, lower like closing a book. Confirm both fences touch before full contact — tape gives one attempt.
  4. Press over the land centroid through a foam pad (tape: 15 s firm; SMP: clamp to nub contact + ¼ turn, 24 h in jig). Never press the overhang — 20 N at the free edge is ~35 MPa in the silicon, at the fracture allowable.
  5. Repeat ×N, then join the ring.
The cure jig, illustrated
The SMP path holds each station in the printed cure jig for 24 h — two fences on one #10-24 rod through the segment's keyhole, wafer edge-captured with zero clamp load on silicon. Paste the one-line description from the product page and the rest of the BOM — nut thread, trap dimensions, bore fit — follows from it. The Ø5 bore in the segment is sized for an M4 clearance fit; if D1 turns out to be M3 or #8-32, that bore diameter needs to change with it.
Square nuts are the right call — and here is the trap that fits one
Your preference is well founded, and the reason is bridging. A captured nut pocket is entered from a side face and printed over, so the slicer has to bridge its ceiling. A square pocket bridges a constant-width flat; a hex pocket's ceiling narrows across the span and its two upper flats become unsupported overhangs, which is why hex traps come out fuzzy and end up needing a reamer. Square also gives more anti-rotation purchase per millimetre of pocket depth.

Trap geometry, driven off the nut you actually buy (s = across flats, m = thickness):
pocket width s + 0.25 (M4 DIN 562 → 7.25 mm) pocket depth m + 0.20 (M4 DIN 562 → 2.40 mm, DIN 557 → 3.40 mm) entry slot s + 0.25 wide, run it to a side face so the nut drops in ceiling ≥ 3 solid layers over the pocket, no infill bore thread + 0.4 clearance, concentric with the pocket
Buy 2: the first one you press in at the wrong depth is a write-off, and nylon nuts strip long before steel does.
Scaling to other configurations
Filament is the only line that moves much. At N=9, θ=10°: Ø150 → 236 g of segments (1 spool), Ø200 → 438 g (1 spool), Ø300 → 1195 g (2 spools), all at 45 % infill, slicer-calibrated — small segments run shell-dominated, so they land above the old uniform-density figures. Wafer count follows N. Tape length is 50 mm per station regardless of wafer size, because pad size is set by the bond physics (OP 015 §4), not by area.
Two pads, not one — and stagger them radially
Two 25 × 25 mm pads give 1250 mm² per station: 18.8 N of sustained shear capacity against 1.25 N applied, a 15× margin. Area was never the constraint. But place one pad near Ri and one near Ro rather than side by side at mid-band — the peel moment (OP 015 §2) acts about the tangential axis, and radial separation is what gives the pad pair any section modulus against it. Two pads at the same radius are ~12× weaker in peel than the full land; staggered, ~6×. Both pass, but the stagger is free.
